01The Stiffness Tell

Stiffness in the first 60 seconds after waking — that "rusty hinge" walk.

The single earliest indicator of joint inflammation is what owners describe as "he just looks stiff for a minute when he gets up." Most owners write this off as old-dog normal. It is not.

What you're seeing is morning synovial-fluid stiffness: overnight, joint lubrication thickens, and the first 30–60 seconds of movement are when an inflamed joint hurts most. Healthy senior dogs don't have this. Inflamed senior dogs do. If you've been seeing it for more than two weeks, the inflammation has been there for months.

What to look for

Watch your dog get up after a nap. Count the seconds before the gait normalizes. More than five seconds, more than once a day, every day? That's a sign.

02The Lick Spot

Excessive licking of one specific paw, hip, or wrist — not a habit, a tell.

Dogs lick what hurts. They've done this for forty thousand years. If your dog has developed a new licking pattern — always the same paw, always the same elbow, always the same hip — that joint is talking to you.

The fur often stains a copper color from saliva oxidation. Owners interpret this as "anxiety licking" or boredom. Vets interpret it as localized chronic pain. The dog is treating the joint the only way it knows how.

03The New Sleeping Shape

A sleeping position you've never seen them use before — usually splayed flat.

Healthy dogs curl. They tuck their hind legs under their body. It's the position that conserves heat and feels safe. When a senior dog stops doing this and starts sleeping splayed flat with their back legs straight out behind them ("the frog position") or lying flat on a cool floor instead of their bed, it's because curling now hurts.

This is one of the most reliable signs vets ask about during senior-dog exams, and it's almost always missed by owners because the position itself looks comfortable. It isn't. It's a coping shape.

What to look for

If your dog has stopped using their bed in favor of the kitchen tile, or if they're avoiding the curl, ask your vet about it at the next visit.

04The Threshold Pause

Hesitation at doorways, stairs, and curb edges — even tiny ones.

A dog with healthy joints walks right through a doorway. A dog with inflamed joints pauses for half a second to plan the step. They're calculating the impact. You'll see it most clearly on tile-to-rug transitions, the lip on a sliding door track, or the curb between sidewalk and street.

Owners describe this as "he just gets a little weird at doors lately." What they're describing is anticipatory pain. The dog has learned that this step hurts and is bracing for it.

05The Bunny Hop

"Bunny-hopping" up stairs with both hind legs together.

A healthy dog climbs stairs with alternating back legs. A dog with hip inflammation increasingly uses both back legs together — the "bunny hop." It distributes the load symmetrically across both hips at once and avoids the painful single-leg push.

This pattern is diagnostic for hip dysplasia or advanced hip arthritis in the absence of a recent injury. It's also one of the easiest signs to spot if you watch your dog go up the stairs head-on instead of from behind.

06The Flat Affect

A new flatness — the "checked out" look in the eyes that owners blame on age.

Chronic pain doesn't just hurt. It depresses. Dogs with persistent joint pain often develop a flatter affect — they greet you with less energy, they stop bringing toys, they sleep a little more, they engage a little less. Owners almost universally describe this as "he's just slowing down with age."

It is not slowing down. It is sustained discomfort blunting the emotional system. It can be reversed in a remarkable percentage of dogs by treating the underlying inflammation. Owners often say the eyes came back first — a week or two before the body did.

What to look for

Compare videos from a year ago to today. Watch the tail, the ears, the speed of the greeting. If the dog you remember is dimmer, joint pain is a likely culprit.

07The Tremor

Trembling in the back legs when standing still — especially while eating.

If your senior dog's back legs tremble while they're standing at their food bowl, the muscles are holding a load that hurts. The tremor is your dog's quadriceps fighting to support a hip or knee that's inflamed and unstable.

This sign frequently appears after the dog has been on a low-quality joint chew that's not actually doing anything.
