Why dentists keep telling people to floss differently

by Dr. Marisol Vega, RDH · Wellness Insider · Updated May 12, 2026
★★★★★ 4.8/5 (3,142 reader ratings) · 6 min read

If you've been told to floss every day and still get the "you're not flossing enough" speech at your next cleaning, the problem isn't you. The problem is probably the floss.

Most dental hygienists will quietly admit something: string floss works, but only if you do it right, every day, for the rest of your life. That's a tall order. The American Dental Association's own surveys put consistent daily flossing at somewhere between 16% and 30% of adults — and that's the self-reported number, which is famously generous.

In the last few years, the conversation has shifted. Hygienists are recommending water flossing not as a replacement for the dentist visit, but as a realistic, sustainable substitute for string floss for people who genuinely won't do it otherwise. The reasoning is simple: a tool you'll actually use beats a tool you'll abandon by Wednesday.

Why string floss falls down for most people

String floss is technique-sensitive. You're supposed to curve it around each tooth in a C-shape, slide it just below the gumline, and avoid snapping it down on the papilla (the gum tissue between teeth). Most people pull it straight back and forth like a saw — which can irritate gums and miss the bacteria that actually causes inflammation.

It's also physically awkward in molars, painful for anyone with crowns or bridges, and basically impossible if you wear traditional braces or a permanent retainer. None of this is news to dental professionals; it's why their drawers are stocked with single-use plastic flossers, interdental brushes, threaders, and yes — countertop water flossers.

What water flossing actually does

A water flosser uses a stream of pulsing water — usually around 1,200–1,400 pulses per minute — to lift food debris and disrupt the bacterial biofilm that builds up between teeth and around the gumline. Clinical reviews (including a 2020 review in the Journal of Clinical Periodontology) have found water flossers reduce gingival bleeding and plaque indices comparably to string floss for most adults, and noticeably better than string floss for people with orthodontic appliances or implants.

Translation: if your hygienist sees bleeding gums at your cleaning, switching from "I keep meaning to floss" to a 30-second pass with a water flosser usually moves that number in the right direction within a few weeks.

The travel problem (and why most water flossers don't solve it)

The old generation of water flossers were countertop units. Big base, big reservoir, dedicated bathroom outlet. They worked, but they didn't travel, and if you spent a week away from home, your gums noticed.

The newer generation is different. Pen-style cordless flossers like the DentaStream are USB-rechargeable, fit in a toiletry bag, and hold enough water for a full session in their internal reservoir. The pressure is comparable to a countertop unit on its lower settings — which, for the record, is what most dentists recommend you use anyway.

What we'd actually recommend

If you're going to upgrade your routine — and especially if you're someone whose dentist has been gently nudging you for years — there are three things worth getting in one go, because they cover the three things string floss alone doesn't:

1. A pen-style water flosser for the daily between-teeth clean. 2. A sonic toothbrush for the surface cleaning that manual brushing under-delivers on. 3. A UV sanitizing case if you travel or share a bathroom — your brush head picks up surprising amounts of bacteria from the air alone.

You don't need all three. But if you're already replacing your routine, replacing it once is cheaper than replacing it three times.
