Why renters and homeowners are quietly skipping the security contract — and using $40 cameras instead

For decades, "home security" meant one thing: a contract with a monitoring company, a panel by the door, and a sticker in the window. Those systems still exist. But over the last few years, a lot of people — especially renters, first-time homeowners, and folks who travel a few weekends a month — have figured out they don't actually need most of it.

What's replacing the contract isn't a "smarter" monitoring service. It's the opposite — people are buying one or two WiFi cameras, sticking a couple of door sensors on their entry points, and running the whole thing from their phone. No installer. No long-term agreement. No monthly bill, unless they want one.

The honest reason: most break-ins are not the burglary-thriller scenario the legacy industry markets against. Most are quick, opportunistic, and from the front. The people deterring them aren't paying $50 a month — they're getting a phone alert and a recording, and that's enough.

"I live in a one-bedroom apartment. A panel by the door felt like overkill. A camera on my bookshelf was enough."

What's actually changed

Three things made standalone cameras genuinely useful, not just cheap:

  1. 1. The cameras stopped being terrible.

    Cheap WiFi cameras used to mean fuzzy 480p, broken apps, and a security risk all by themselves. The current generation does 1080p+, has working night vision, encrypted cloud storage, and the apps are fine. You're not getting a Ring Protect Pro experience — you're getting something that works, costs less than dinner, and shows you what's happening at home from your phone.

  2. 2. Door and window sensors got dumb-simple.

    The original alarm industry made contact sensors feel like an installation project. The new versions are two pieces of plastic with adhesive backs. You stick one on the door, one on the frame, and your phone tells you when they separate. Coin-cell battery lasts about a year. That's it.

  3. 3. The phone alert replaced the dispatcher.

    Old systems sold the monitoring center as the value. Now, the alert hits your pocket in under a second. For a lot of people that's a more useful response than waiting for someone in a call center to phone you and ask if you're okay. You see the video, you make the call.

Where standalone cameras work — and where they don't

We want to be straight with you: this isn't the answer for everyone.

If you live in a high-risk area, run a business out of your home, or already have a system you trust, keep it. Monitored systems with battery backup and cellular failover do something a WiFi camera can't — they keep working if power and internet go out.

But for most renters, condo owners, and people in townhomes and single-family houses in normal neighborhoods — the calculus has changed. The honest job a camera does is:

  • Tell you something is happening when you're not there.
  • Show you who it is.
  • Keep a recording in case you need it later.

That's about 90% of what most people actually want from "security."

What a reasonable setup looks like

We've been recommending the same starter mix to people who ask:

  • One indoor WiFi camera placed somewhere with a wide view — a living room or hallway facing the front door.
  • A door or window sensor or two on whatever your "weak entry point" is — front door if you're a renter, back door or sliding door if you're in a house.
  • Optional: a doorbell camera at the front if you get a lot of packages or unknown visitors.

That's it. No installer. No drilling. Plug in the camera, peel-and-stick the sensors, set up the app, done — most people are running in under twenty minutes.

The honest economics

A traditional monitored system runs $30–$60 a month, often with a 36-month contract. Over three years that's $1,000–$2,000, not counting equipment. A starter kit with a camera and a couple of sensors is a one-time spend in the $40–$100 range and works with the WiFi you already pay for. You can add a cloud recording plan later if you want — but most people don't.
